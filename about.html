I'm an AI/ML engineer based in Portland, Maine. I build systems that take machine learning from proof-of-concept to production — the full stack of getting an AI idea to actually work inside an organization at scale.

My background is in physics and mathematics. I studied both at the University of Maine, graduating magna cum laude in 2019 with a double major, and spent a semester abroad studying advanced algebra and dynamical systems at the American University of Bulgaria. Before that, I attended the Maine School of Science and Mathematics. The combination gave me a way of thinking about problems — structurally, from first principles — that I've found more useful in engineering than I expected.

My career has been eclectic by design. I've built quantitative models for a Hong Kong hedge fund (Sylebra Capital), served as CTO of a sustainability startup (Muuse) that won the OpenIDEO NextGen Cup Challenge and landed partnerships with McDonald's and Starbucks, contributed to open-source animal communication research at the Earth Species Project, built data infrastructure and ML systems at a sustainability scoring company (Choosefinch), and most recently led AI engineering at WEX Inc., where I built the generative AI platform and the claims automation system that became a flagship example of applied AI in production.

The WEX work is what I'm most proud of technically. I took a notebook-based prototype — a direct LLM API call — and rebuilt it as a fully productionized microservice: FastAPI with Pydantic validation, LangGraph for multi-step reasoning, containerized on Kubernetes with Helm, CI/CD via GitHub Actions, Terraform-managed infrastructure across Azure and AWS. The claims processing system achieved 97% accuracy and reduced processing time from days to seconds. More importantly, it worked well enough that I used it as the basis for building a broader AI application platform — a standardized framework that let teams across the org move from experimental models to production services in a fraction of the previous time.

Outside of work, I've spent a lot of time in the mountains. I've climbed El Capitan in Yosemite, and I hold the Maine state record for the longest highline ever sent. Highlining is an interesting engineering problem on top of everything else — I've worked out elastic catenary equations for anchor system forces, because knowing the math meant I could make better decisions about safety margins. The physics degree turned out to be useful.

I've also spent years doing mathematical visualization as a hobby — over 7 years of sketches in Processing exploring fractals, geometric transformations, and dynamical systems. Some of that work, particularly the IFS fractal encoder and the linkage genetic algorithm, became projects I'm genuinely proud of independent of any professional context.

A few things I care about: finding the unexpected depth in things that look simple. Working with people who are actually excited about what they're building. And the idea that rigorous thinking and genuine curiosity are the same skill.
